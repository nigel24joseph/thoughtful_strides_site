For individuals

EMDR Therapy

Helps with: trauma and PTSD, anxiety and phobias, depression, grief and loss, first responder trauma, relationship triggers

EMDR stands for Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing. It was developed in the late 1980s and is now one of the most researched therapies for trauma, recognised in international and Australian treatment guidelines for PTSD. It's also used for anxiety, phobias, grief, depression and other difficulties linked to distressing experiences.

When something overwhelming happens, the memory doesn't always get processed the way everyday memories do. It can stay stored with the original emotions, images, body sensations and beliefs, such as "I'm not safe" or "It was my fault". That's why a smell, a sound or a situation can bring it all flooding back, as if it's happening now.

How EMDR helps

EMDR uses eye movements, or another left and right rhythm such as tapping, while you briefly bring the memory to mind. This helps your brain do what it would naturally do: file the memory away as something that happened in the past. You still remember what happened, but it no longer carries the same charge, and beliefs like "I'm not safe" can shift to "It's over, and I'm safe now".

What EMDR Sessions Are Like

EMDR follows a clear, structured process. Much of the safety in EMDR comes from the time spent before any memory work begins, so we never rush into it.

1

Getting Ready

Before any memory work, I take time to understand your history and check whether EMDR is right for you, and when.

We work out together which experiences are driving what you're feeling now, and plan where to start. I'll also help you build ways to steady yourself, such as calming and grounding techniques, so you have tools you can use in sessions and in everyday life. This stage takes as long as it needs to.

Read more ↓
2

Processing

You bring a memory to mind and move your eyes from side to side in short sets, simply noticing whatever comes up.

Between sets, we pause and you tell me briefly what you noticed. You don't need to describe the memory in detail, and you stay in control the whole time, with a stop signal you can use whenever you need it. I watch closely how you're going and adjust as we work. If things become intense, you won't be left alone with it.

Read more ↓
3

Settling and Moving Forward

Every session ends with you feeling settled, and you'll know what to expect between sessions.

At the start of the next session, we check in on how things have been and what's changed. As memories are processed, we also look ahead, practising how you'd like to feel and respond in situations that used to trigger you.

Before we begin

EMDR is well researched, and how it's delivered makes a real difference. Before any memory work, I take time to understand your history and check whether EMDR is right for you, and when. Together we work out where to start, and make sure you have ways to steady yourself first.

During sessions

I watch closely how you're going and pace the work to what you can manage. If things become intense, you won't be left alone with it, and every session ends with you feeling settled.

How I Keep EMDR Safe

Most difficulties people have with EMDR come from how it's delivered, not from the therapy itself. Here's how I take care at each stage:

  1. 1

    Before we start

    Getting ready, so memory work feels safe

    • We build stability and ways to calm yourself first, and take as long as that needs

      Avoids starting memory work before someone feels steady enough

    • We plan together which memories to work on and where to start, tracing today's reactions back to where they began, and building up to the hardest ones when you're ready

      Avoids working on the wrong memories, or in the wrong order, such as starting with the most painful one, or missing the earlier experiences behind what's happening now

    • I check for this before we begin, and adjust the pace if it comes up

      Avoids not checking whether someone tends to feel spaced out or disconnected

  2. 2

    During sessions

    Watching closely and adjusting as we go

    • I watch closely and slow down, pause or change approach when needed

      Avoids pushing on when someone is overwhelmed

    • I check in regularly, so shutting down isn't mistaken for progress

      Avoids missing when someone has gone quiet or numb, which can look like calm

    • I help it move forward gently, so you're not left stuck in the memory

      Avoids processing getting stuck and going round in circles

  3. 3

    Ending and between sessions

    Leaving steady and knowing what to expect

    • Every session ends with time to settle, so you leave feeling steady

      Avoids ending a session with someone still stirred up

    • You'll know what's normal, how to manage it, and how to reach me

      Avoids not preparing people for what can happen between sessions

That's why preparation, pacing and experience matter so much.

Honest Expectations

What You Might Notice Between Sessions

EMDR keeps working after the session ends. Most of these effects are short lived and a sign that your mind is processing. I'll prepare you for them before we start, and you can get in touch if anything feels like too much.

Call 0479 171 121
  • Feeling more emotional or tired for a day or two
  • Vivid dreams, or memories and thoughts coming to mind
  • New insights about yourself or what happened
  • Sometimes feeling a little worse before you feel better
  • Feeling lighter or calmer than you expected
  • Old triggers losing some of their intensity

When We Might Go More Gently

Sometimes it's better to spend longer preparing, or to wait before memory work. This might be the case if you're going through a crisis, using alcohol or drugs to cope, or often feel spaced out or disconnected. This doesn't mean EMDR isn't for you. It means we build a stronger foundation first.

Things to Let Me Know

Let me know if you're pregnant, or have a heart condition, epilepsy or an eye condition, so we can adapt the approach, for example using tapping instead of eye movements. If you're involved in a court case related to what happened, tell me before we begin, as EMDR can change how a memory is recalled.

For individuals

Radical Exposure Tapping (RET)

Helps with: complex trauma, strong emotional reactions to reminders, and when eye movements aren't the right fit

Radical Exposure Tapping (RET) is an Australian approach developed by Dr Laurie MacKinnon. It draws on EMDR and combines it with gentle tapping on points on the face and hands, while you briefly bring a memory to mind. I've practised RET since 2014, and I use it as a complement to EMDR rather than a replacement.

When I Use RET

When Eye Movements Don't Suit

Some people find eye movements uncomfortable, tiring or hard to follow. Tapping gives us another way to work with the same memories, without changing the careful preparation and pacing.

Complex Trauma

When there are many experiences tangled together, RET lets us target specific memories and the reactions linked to them more closely, one piece at a time.

As with EMDR, you don't need to describe what happened in detail, you stay in control the whole time, and every session ends with you feeling settled.

For individuals

Schema Therapy

Helps with: long standing patterns, complex trauma, relationship patterns that keep repeating

Schema Therapy was developed by Dr Jeffrey Young for difficulties that run deep and keep coming back. It brings together ideas from cognitive behaviour therapy, attachment theory and experiential therapies.

Schemas are deep patterns, made up of memories, emotions and beliefs about yourself and others, that formed early in life. They develop when your need to feel safe, supported, or free to be yourself wasn't fully met growing up. Even though you're an adult now, these patterns tend to keep repeating, shaping how you see yourself and how you react in relationships, at work and in everyday life.

Patterns Schema Therapy Can Help With

These are some of the most common schemas. You might recognise one or two, or several.

Abandonment

Fear of Being Left

Worrying that the people you love will leave, or holding on tightly in relationships.

Defectiveness and Shame

Feeling Not Good Enough

A deep sense of being flawed, unlovable, or ashamed of who you are.

Mistrust

Finding It Hard to Trust

Expecting others to hurt you, take advantage of you, or let you down.

Emotional Deprivation

Feeling Emotionally Alone

Believing no one will really understand you or be there for you.

Unrelenting Standards

Never Measuring Up

Pushing yourself to meet impossibly high standards, however much you achieve.

Self Sacrifice

Always Putting Others First

Giving up your own needs to keep others happy or avoid conflict.

Better Together

How EMDR and Schema Therapy Work Together

For many people, especially with complex trauma, I use EMDR and Schema Therapy side by side. Schema Therapy helps us understand the patterns driving how you feel and react, and trace them back to the memories that shaped them, so we know what to target in processing.

EMDR then helps process those memories, so the patterns lose their hold. As the old charge settles, new ways of seeing yourself and responding to others become easier to live by, not just understand.

The past shaped you, but it doesn't have to keep deciding how you feel today.
For couples

Gottman Method

Helps with: ongoing conflict, communication problems, emotional distance, rebuilding trust

The Gottman Method is built on decades of research into what actually predicts whether a relationship thrives or breaks down. Rather than treating every disagreement as a one-off, it looks at the specific habits and blind spots you've built up together over time, and gives us practical, tested ways to shift them.

What We Work On

Love Maps

Rebuilding a detailed, up to date picture of each other's inner world, through simple guided questions about daily life, worries, hopes and history.

Conflict Blueprint

Mapping how an argument actually unfolds between you, what triggers it, how it escalates, and where either of you could step in differently.

Stress Reducing Conversation

Supporting each other after a hard day without it turning into another fight, by listening and validating before jumping to advice.

Five Essential Rituals

Small, repeated moments of connection that quietly hold a relationship together, like how you greet each other, say goodbye or check in.

Art of Compromise

Finding workable middle ground on issues that never seem to resolve, so compromise feels fair rather than one sided.

For couples

Emotionally Focused Couples Therapy

Helps with: feeling disconnected, the same argument on repeat, one partner pursuing while the other withdraws

Emotionally Focused Couples Therapy (EFCT) is an attachment based approach. Rather than focusing on the argument itself, it looks at the emotional cycle underneath it: the moment one of you reaches for connection and the other pulls back, or one raises their voice and the other shuts down. Slowing that cycle down helps you see what's underneath it, often hurt, fear, or a need to feel close again.

What We Work On

Understanding Attachment

Looking at the emotional fears driving conflict. Most conflict is really about needing to feel wanted, safe, or important to each other.

De-escalating Conflict

Slowing the cycle down in session, so it becomes something you work against together, rather than against each other.

Emotional Safety

Once the cycle settles, showing each other what's underneath it, and having it land gently.

Reaching for Connection

Recognising the moments you're each reaching for reassurance or closeness, even when it looks like anger or distance.

Rebuilding Trust

Creating new, consistent experiences of being there for each other, so trust is rebuilt through action over time.

Gottman, EFCT and EMDR together

Many couples benefit from both couples approaches: Gottman for practical tools, EFCT for the emotions underneath. When old hurts or past trauma keep getting triggered in the relationship, I can also offer individual EMDR sessions alongside our couples work.
