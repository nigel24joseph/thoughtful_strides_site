Freedom From Fear and Worry

Anxiety is one of the most treatable mental health conditions. You don't have to manage this on your own.

Anxiety is Australia's most common mental health condition, affecting around 1 in 4 people at some point in their lives. It can show up as constant worry, sudden panic attacks, intrusive thoughts and compulsions, or a persistent sense of dread that's hard to explain.

I help you understand what's driving your anxiety and build practical, evidence-based tools to feel more in control again. Anxiety disorders cover a range of presentations, including generalised anxiety (GAD), panic disorder, social anxiety, phobias, and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), and they often overlap with each other. Whatever form it takes, effective, well-evidenced treatment is available.

Anxiety shrinks the world down to what feels safe. Therapy is about widening it back out again, one manageable step at a time.

Panic Therapy and Counselling

Panic disorder involves recurrent, unexpected panic attacks, sudden surges of intense fear accompanied by physical symptoms such as a racing heart, chest tightness, or dizziness, along with ongoing worry about having another attack. It's highly treatable, and many people fear something is physically wrong with them before recognising the pattern as panic.

OCD Therapy and Counselling

OCD involves intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images, or urges (obsessions) that cause significant distress, and repetitive behaviours or mental acts (compulsions) performed to reduce that distress or prevent a feared outcome. Common themes include contamination, harm, symmetry, or intrusive taboo thoughts. OCD responds well to structured, evidence-based treatment.

Phobia Therapy and Counselling

A phobia is an intense fear of a specific thing or situation, far bigger than the actual risk, such as flying, driving, needles, the dentist, heights or vomiting. Avoiding it brings relief but makes the fear grow. Phobias are among the most treatable forms of anxiety, and EMDR can help process where the fear began.

When Your Mind Stays on Guard

EMDR Therapy for Anxiety, Panic, OCD and Phobias

Anxiety often makes sense when you trace it back. A mind that's always scanning for danger has usually learnt, at some point, that it needed to. Knowing you're safe now doesn't always switch that alarm off, because the feeling is tied to earlier experiences rather than to what's happening today.

When EMDR may help

  • Panic attacks. Sudden waves of fear, especially if you can remember when the first one happened.
  • Constant worry. A sense that something bad will happen unless you stay alert.
  • Specific fears and phobias. Flying, driving, needles, medical or dental procedures, or social situations that feel bigger than they should.
  • Beliefs like "I'm not safe" or "I'm not in control". Thoughts that drive checking, avoiding, or needing reassurance.
  • Anxiety after a difficult event. Feeling on edge since an accident, illness, or frightening experience.

How EMDR helps

EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitisation and Reprocessing) helps process the experiences that taught your nervous system to stay on guard. As they settle, the alarm doesn't need to sound as often. EMDR can also target the situations you fear in the future, so you can face them feeling steadier.

We start by building ways to calm your body and mind, so you have tools to use in sessions and in daily life. EMDR processing only begins once you feel ready.

For OCD, EMDR usually works alongside other approaches rather than on its own. We'll talk through what's likely to help most in your situation.

Is EMDR Safe?

EMDR is well researched, and how it's delivered makes the difference. Most difficulties come from starting memory work before someone feels steady enough, choosing the wrong memories to work on, missing signs of overwhelm, or ending a session without settling things. I take time to prepare you, pace each session to what you can manage, and make sure you leave settled.

A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear accompanied by physical symptoms such as a racing heart, shortness of breath, chest tightness, or dizziness, typically peaking within minutes. Panic disorder involves recurrent, unexpected panic attacks along with ongoing worry about having another one.

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD) involves intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images, or urges (obsessions) that cause distress, and repetitive behaviours or mental acts (compulsions) performed to reduce that distress or prevent a feared outcome. Common themes include contamination, harm, symmetry, or intrusive taboo thoughts.

A phobia is an intense, persistent fear of a specific thing or situation, such as flying, driving, needles, heights, animals or vomiting, that feels far bigger than the actual risk. Many people know the fear doesn't make sense but still feel their body react, and start avoiding the situation, which tends to make the fear stronger over time.

The most well-evidenced approach to OCD is Cognitive Behavioural Therapy that includes gradual, structured exposure work, often combined with Acceptance and Commitment Therapy or Schema Therapy where longstanding patterns are involved.
